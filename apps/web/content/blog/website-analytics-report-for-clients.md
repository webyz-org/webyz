# How to create a website analytics report for clients

If you manage websites or marketing campaigns for clients, reporting is probably a regular part of your work.

The difficult part isn't collecting data. Tools like Google Analytics can give you more numbers than you could ever fit into a monthly report. The real challenge is deciding which numbers actually matter to your client.

A good website analytics report shouldn't leave someone wondering what all those charts mean. By the time they finish reading it, they should understand three things:

- How their website is performing
- What has changed since the last reporting period
- What they should do next

In this guide, we'll walk through how to create a website analytics report that clients can actually understand and use.

## Start with the client's goals

Before opening Google Analytics or building a dashboard, take a step back and ask a simple question:

**What is this client trying to achieve with their website?**

The answer will determine what belongs in the report.

For an ecommerce business, revenue and purchases might be the most important metrics. For a service business, you may care more about contact form submissions, phone calls, or quote requests.

A blog or media website may be focused on growing organic traffic and engagement.

This sounds obvious, but it's easy to forget when you have access to dozens of metrics. Including every available number doesn't make a report more useful. In most cases, it just makes it harder to read.

Your report should connect website performance to the goals the client actually cares about. Customizing reports around the stakeholder's needs and business objectives is also a key part of making analytics useful rather than overwhelming.

## Choose the metrics that actually matter

Every business is different, but there are a few metrics that work well as a starting point for most website reports.

### 1. Website traffic

Start with the basics: how many people are visiting the website?

You might include metrics such as:

- Active users
- Sessions
- New and returning visitors

Don't just show the number for the current month. Compare it with the previous period so the client can immediately see whether traffic is moving in the right direction.

For example:

> The website received 12,450 visitors this month, up 18% compared with last month.

That tells a much clearer story than simply placing "12,450" in a large box.

### 2. Where visitors are coming from

Knowing how many people visit the website is useful. Knowing where they came from is even more useful.

Break traffic down into channels such as:

- Organic search
- Direct traffic
- Paid campaigns
- Social media
- Referral websites

This can help answer questions like:

**Is our SEO work bringing in more visitors?**

**Is the latest advertising campaign working?**

**Which marketing channels are worth investing in?**

Google Analytics provides reporting around acquisition and other parts of the customer journey, and these reports can be tailored to show the information most relevant to a team or business.

### 3. Top-performing pages

Your client should know which pages are doing the most work for their business.

Look at the pages that attract the most visitors and consider what they have in common. Are they ranking well in search? Are they linked from a successful campaign? Do they answer a question people are actively searching for?

GA4's Pages and screens report can help identify the pages visitors view and includes metrics such as active users, views, engagement time, and key events.

You don't need to include a list of every page on the website. Focus on the pages that tell an interesting story.

For example:

> Our guide to choosing a travel destination became the site's most visited page this month, bringing in 32% more traffic from Google search.

That gives the client both the number and the reason it matters.

### 4. Engagement

Traffic alone doesn't tell you whether people are finding the website useful.

That's where engagement metrics come in.

Depending on your goals, you might look at:

- Engagement rate
- Average engagement time
- Pages viewed
- Important user actions

These numbers can help you understand what happens after someone arrives on the website.

However, don't get too caught up in reporting every engagement metric. A report should help the client make decisions, not turn into an analytics textbook.

### 5. Conversions and key actions

For many businesses, this is the most important section of the report.

What do you actually want visitors to do?

That could be:

- Submit a contact form
- Make a purchase
- Request a quote
- Sign up for a newsletter
- Book an appointment

Track the actions that have real value to the business and show how they changed over time.

Google Analytics uses events to measure user actions, and important events can be marked as key events for reporting purposes.

## Keep the report simple

One of the biggest mistakes in client reporting is trying to show everything.

A dashboard with 20 charts might look impressive, but it can quickly become overwhelming, especially for clients who don't work with analytics every day.

A better approach is to lead with the most important information.

A simple report structure could look like this:

### 1. Performance summary

Start with a short overview of what happened during the reporting period.

For example:

> Website traffic increased by 15% this month, with organic search contributing most of the growth. Lead submissions also increased, although the contact page saw lower engagement than usual.

This gives the client the main story before they look at any charts.

### 2. Key numbers

Show the most important metrics for the business.

For example:

- Website visitors
- Traffic growth
- Leads or purchases
- Conversion rate

### 3. Traffic sources

Show where visitors are coming from and how each channel is performing.

### 4. Top pages and content

Highlight the pages that performed particularly well, or poorly.

### 5. Insights and next steps

This is the section many reports forget.

Don't just tell the client what happened. Explain what you think should happen next.

For example:

> Organic traffic is growing steadily, so we recommend creating more content around the topics driving the most search traffic.

Or:

> The advertising campaign generated a high number of visitors but relatively few enquiries. We recommend reviewing the landing page and the campaign targeting.

A useful report should translate numbers into decisions, with the most important insights presented clearly rather than buried beneath charts.

## Use the same format every month

Once you've created a report structure that works, try to keep it consistent.

Clients shouldn't have to learn how to read a completely new report every month.

Using the same format makes it easier to compare performance over time. It also saves you from rebuilding the report from scratch whenever the reporting period comes around.

You can still add a new section when something important happens. But the core structure should remain familiar.

## Don't send data without context

Here's a simple rule to remember:

**Every important number should answer a question.**

Instead of writing:

> Organic traffic: 8,200 visitors

Try:

> Organic traffic increased by 22%, mainly because two recently published articles started ranking on Google.

The second version gives the client something they can understand and act on.

Your job isn't just to collect analytics data. It's to help the client understand what that data means.

A good website analytics report doesn't need dozens of charts or complicated metrics.
